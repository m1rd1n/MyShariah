SECTION 7: SHARIAH COMPLIANCE REQUIREMENTS FOR MURABAHA CONTRACTS

7.1 PROHIBITION OF RIBA (INTEREST)

7.1.1 Any predetermined, guaranteed, or fixed return on a financial
transaction that is not linked to an actual asset sale or service is
classified as riba and is strictly prohibited under Islamic law.

7.1.2 In a Murabaha financing arrangement, the profit margin (ribh) must
be agreed upon and fixed at the time of contract inception. The profit
rate shall not be subject to revision, adjustment, or indexation to any
external benchmark including but not limited to:
  (a) Kuala Lumpur Interbank Offered Rate (KLIBOR)
  (b) Base Financing Rate (BFR)
  (c) Overnight Policy Rate (OPR)
  (d) Any floating rate mechanism

7.1.3 Any clause that provides for a variable, adjustable, or floating
profit rate in a Murabaha contract shall render the contract non-compliant
with this Framework. The Bank must fix the selling price (comprising cost
price plus profit margin) at contract execution, and this price shall
remain unchanged for the duration of the financing tenure.

7.1.4 The disclosure of cost price and profit margin is mandatory in all
Murabaha contracts. Non-disclosure or bundling of cost and profit into a
single undisclosed amount constitutes gharar and is not permitted.

REFERENCE: BNM/RH/PD 029-1, Para 7.1 — Fixed Profit Rate Requirement


7.2 CONSTRUCTIVE POSSESSION AND ASSET OWNERSHIP

7.2.1 A fundamental requirement of Murabaha is that the financier (bank)
must have ownership and bear the risk of the asset before it can be sold
to the customer. This is known as constructive possession (qabd hukmi).

7.2.2 The bank must demonstrate that it has:
  (a) Entered into a binding purchase agreement with the supplier
  (b) Assumed the risk of loss or damage to the asset
  (c) Held legal or beneficial title to the asset, however briefly

7.2.3 Direct delivery arrangements (where the supplier delivers the asset
directly to the customer without physical passage through the bank) are
permissible ONLY if the bank has first completed a valid purchase contract
with the supplier and has assumed ownership risk prior to the on-sale to
the customer.

7.2.4 Any arrangement where the customer takes delivery of the asset
BEFORE the bank has formally purchased it from the supplier constitutes
bay' al-ma'dum (sale of a non-existent asset) and is void under Shariah.

7.2.5 The sequence of events must be:
  Step 1: Bank purchases from supplier (or executes purchase agreement)
  Step 2: Bank assumes ownership risk
  Step 3: Bank sells to customer via Murabaha contract
  Step 4: Customer takes delivery

REFERENCE: BNM/RH/PD 029-4, Para 8.2 — Constructive Possession Requirement


7.3 PROHIBITION OF GHARAR (EXCESSIVE UNCERTAINTY)

7.3.1 A Murabaha contract must not contain excessive ambiguity or
uncertainty (gharar fahish) in any of its material terms. All key
contractual terms must be known, certain, and agreed upon by both parties
at the time of execution.

7.3.2 Material terms that must be certain and fully disclosed include:
  (a) The asset being sold — must be clearly identified, described,
      and in existence at the time of contract
  (b) Cost price — the price paid by the bank to acquire the asset
  (c) Profit margin — the bank's profit, expressed as a fixed amount
      or percentage agreed at contract inception
  (d) Total selling price — sum of cost price and profit margin
  (e) Payment schedule — all installment amounts and due dates
  (f) Tenure — the financing period with clear start and end dates

7.3.3 Vague or open-ended terms that leave any material element to
future determination constitute gharar and are not permitted.

7.3.4 In cross-border Murabaha transactions, uncertainty arising from
conflicting legal frameworks or undetermined governing law constitutes
a form of gharar. The contract must specify a single, clearly identified
governing law and dispute resolution mechanism.

REFERENCE: BNM/RH/PD 029-3, Para 7.3 — Certainty of Contract Terms


7.4 PROHIBITION OF MAYSIR (SPECULATION)

7.4.1 A Murabaha contract must not contain elements that introduce
speculative outcomes. Contractual obligations must not be contingent
upon uncertain future events that either party cannot control.

7.4.2 Penalty or acceleration clauses that are triggered by market
movements, commodity prices, or external indices introduce an element
of maysir and must be avoided.

PART B: LATE PAYMENT AND TA'WIDH GUIDELINES
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

SECTION: BNM/RH/PD 029-7 — COMPENSATION FOR LATE PAYMENT (TA'WIDH)

1. DEFINITION AND PERMISSIBILITY

1.1 Ta'widh refers to compensation that may be imposed on a customer for
actual losses suffered by the bank as a direct result of the customer's
late payment or default in a Murabaha financing arrangement.

1.2 Ta'widh is permissible under Shariah subject to strict conditions.
It must not be used as a profit-generating mechanism for the bank.

2. CONDITIONS FOR IMPOSING TA'WIDH

2.1 The compensation imposed must not exceed the actual financial loss
incurred by the bank as a result of the late payment. Charging in excess
of actual loss is equivalent to riba and is prohibited.

2.2 The rate of ta'widh shall not exceed 1% per annum on the outstanding
amount, or the actual loss incurred, whichever is lower.

2.3 CRITICAL REQUIREMENT — DISPOSITION OF TA'WIDH:
Any ta'widh collected from the customer MUST be channelled to a charity
(baitulmal or equivalent charitable fund). It is strictly prohibited for
the bank to recognise ta'widh as income in its financial statements.

2.4 Any late payment clause that:
  (a) Sets charges as a percentage accruing monthly (effectively compound)
  (b) Allows the bank to recognise charges as income or profit
  (c) Exceeds the actual documented loss of the bank
  — shall be deemed non-compliant with this guideline and void.

2.5 The contract must explicitly state that late payment charges are for
compensation purposes only and will be donated to charity.

REFERENCE: BNM/RH/PD 029-7 — Ta'widh and Gharamah Guidelines

PART C: CROSS-BORDER AND MULTI-JURISDICTION REQUIREMENTS
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

SECTION: AAOIFI SHARIAH STANDARD NO. 8 — MURABAHA (CROSS-BORDER)

1. JURISDICTIONAL CLARITY

1.1 In Murabaha contracts that involve parties or assets in multiple
legal jurisdictions, the contract must clearly specify:
  (a) A single primary governing law
  (b) A single designated dispute resolution forum
  (c) The Shariah supervisory authority with oversight responsibility

1.2 Dual jurisdiction clauses that allow either party to elect between
competing forums at their sole discretion introduce gharar into the
dispute resolution process and are not permissible.

1.3 Where the applicable governing law of a foreign jurisdiction conflicts
with Shariah principles, Shariah principles shall prevail. The contract
must contain an explicit Shariah supremacy clause to this effect.

2. SHARIAH BOARD AUTHORITY IN CROSS-BORDER TRANSACTIONS

2.1 For transactions governed by Malaysian law but involving cross-border
elements, Bank Negara Malaysia's Shariah Advisory Council (SAC) rulings
shall be the primary reference for Shariah matters.

2.2 Where AAOIFI standards apply (e.g., transactions with GCC counterparties),
any conflict between AAOIFI standards and SAC rulings must be resolved by
escalation to both bodies, not unilaterally by the bank.

REFERENCE: AAOIFI Shariah Standard No. 8, Clauses 1.3 and 2.4

PART D: DEFAULT AND ASSET REPOSSESSION
═══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

SECTION: BNM/RH/PD 029-6 — DEFAULT, ACCELERATION AND REPOSSESSION

1. ACCELERATION OF OUTSTANDING BALANCE

1.1 Upon default, a bank may accelerate the entire outstanding selling
price (baki harga jualan) subject to the following conditions:
  (a) The default event is clearly defined in the contract
  (b) The customer is given reasonable notice and opportunity to remedy

1.2 The accelerated amount must be the original outstanding selling price
only. The bank may not add additional profit or interest to the accelerated
amount — doing so constitutes riba.

2. REPOSSESSION AND SHORTFALL RECOVERY

2.1 Upon repossession of the financed asset, the bank must conduct a fair
market valuation. Any proceeds from the sale of the repossessed asset
must be applied to reduce the outstanding selling price.

2.2 Recovery of a shortfall (the difference between outstanding selling
price and repossession proceeds) from the customer's personal assets is
permissible provided:
  (a) The shortfall is calculated on the outstanding selling price only
  (b) No additional charges, penalties, or accrued interest are included
      in the shortfall calculation

REFERENCE: BNM/RH/PD 029-6 — Default and Recovery Guidelines
